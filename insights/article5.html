The $15 Million Exemption and the 1,000-Year Trust: Why 2026 Changed Florida Estate Planning

The federal estate exemption is now $15 million per person, permanent, and Florida allows trusts to run 1,000 years. The planning question flipped from how much to shelter to how long it can stay sheltered.
Prime Domain Capital · November 2026 · 6 min read

In the autumn of 2026, a Naples estate planning attorney took a call she had been expecting for months. The client, a founder who had sold his company the previous year, had one question: with the federal estate tax exemption now set at $15 million per person, did he still need the dynasty trust they had spent a year designing? Her answer surprised him. The exemption had made the trust less urgent as a tax shelter. It had made it more valuable as a structure. The question was no longer how much wealth he could move out of his estate before a deadline. It was how long the wealth could stay protected, governed, and out of the hands of future creditors, divorces, and bad decisions.

That shift is the story of Florida estate planning in 2026, and it is why the Gulf Coast trust business is having a different kind of boom than the one that filled the headlines in 2024 and 2025.

The exemption that stopped being a deadline

For a decade, American estate planning ran on a countdown. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act had doubled the federal estate, gift, and generation-skipping transfer exemption, but the doubling was scheduled to sunset at the end of 2025, dropping the exemption back to roughly $7 million per person in 2026. Families with taxable estates spent years rushing gifts, funding trusts, and using exemption before it vanished. The 2025 federal tax legislation, P.L. 119-21, ended the countdown: the exemption is $15 million per person, $30 million per married couple, beginning in 2026, made permanent and indexed for inflation. For 2027, the Internal Revenue Service set it at $15.48 million per person in Revenue Procedure 2026-35.

The practical effect is that very few families now owe federal estate tax at all. A couple can shelter $30 million. The planning conversation has moved down the wealth scale in one sense and up it in another: fewer families need exemption planning, but the families who do need it are planning for something larger than a tax bill. They are planning for control across generations, and Florida is where that planning increasingly happens.

Florida's advantage is duration. For trusts created on or after July 1, 2022, Florida law allows a trust to last 1,000 years under Fla. Stat. section 689.225, up from 360 years for earlier trusts. A trust created in 2026 can, in principle, govern family wealth until the year 3026. Florida charges no state income tax on private trusts, no state estate tax, and no inheritance tax. It has a directed trust act that lets a family split investment, distribution, and administrative functions among different parties, a modern decanting statute that lets trustees modify an irrevocable trust as circumstances change, and a homestead exemption in the state constitution that shields a primary residence of unlimited value from nearly all creditors.

The market underneath the law

The legal framework is meeting a housing market that has found its footing. The Naples Area Board of REALTORS reported 623 closed sales in September 2026, up 2.5% from a year earlier, with an overall median closed price of $575,000, up 4.5%. Inventory fell 17.3% to 4,240 properties, or 5.5 months of supply, and the average days on market improved to 99 from 107. Single-family homes closed at a median $747,500, up 3.1%, while condos came in at $400,000, down 2.4%. The October figures extended the pattern: the Naples median closed price reached $598,500, up 4.1% year over year, on 569 closed sales.

The insurance bill that haunted the migration for three years has also turned. The Florida Office of Insurance Regulation reported that homeowners insurance rates fell in 51 of Florida's 67 counties in 2026. Citizens Property Insurance, the state-created insurer of last resort, recommended a statewide average rate cut of about 2.6% for 2026, its first decrease since 2015, and reported 216,577 policies in force as of October 23, 2026, down from a peak of 1,407,805 in September 2023. The average owner-occupied homeowners policy in force in Florida cost $3,675.31 a year as of May 31, 2026, according to the Office's Market Intelligence Report, computed from filings covering 4,161,048 policies. The crisis has not vanished, but the direction has reversed, and direction is what movers watch.

For the trust industry, the arithmetic is straightforward. Florida Trust Wealth Management, headquartered in the region, has grown past $5 billion in assets under management. National firms including Northern Trust and Bessemer Trust keep Gulf Coast offices to serve migrated clients. The professionals are the constraint now, not the clients: wait times for initial consultations at top Naples estate planning practices stretch for months.

The counterargument: the fortress has gaps

Florida's reputation as a wealth fortress deserves its caveats. The state does not recognize self-settled asset protection trusts, so a Floridian cannot create a trust for his own benefit and shield it from his own creditors the way a Nevadan or South Dakotan can. Trust income is taxed at the top federal rate of 40.8% above roughly $16,000 in 2026, which is why most dynasty trusts are drafted as grantor trusts, with the grantor paying the income tax personally. And the $15 million exemption removes the tax urgency that drove a decade of trust funding: a family worth $12 million has no federal estate tax problem at all, and a 1,000-year trust for that family is a governance project, not a tax project. Some families will reasonably decide they do not need one.

There is also the question of whether a 1,000-year trust is a promise or a paradox. No trustee, no protector, and no distribution committee can foresee the needs of descendants ten generations out. The decanting statute and trust protector provisions exist precisely because long trusts need escape hatches. A structure designed to last a millennium will be rewritten many times before it gets there, and each rewrite is a chance for the family's intent to drift.

Those caveats do not diminish the shift. They define it. The families who benefit most from a 1,000-year Florida trust in 2026 are not the ones sheltering $15 million from tax. They are the ones with $50 million or $100 million who want the wealth to survive their grandchildren's divorces, lawsuits, and enthusiasms. The exemption made the trust optional for most. It made the trust permanent for the few who need it most.

What this means for the Gulf Coast

Three implications follow. First, situs is now a thousand-year decision, not a tax-year decision. A family that chooses Florida law in 2026 is choosing the jurisdiction that will govern its wealth for longer than any institution in American history has existed. Due diligence on the trustee matters more, not less, when the horizon is that long.

Second, the trust business on the Gulf Coast is becoming a duration business. The firms that win will be the ones that can administer complex directed trusts, run decanting processes cleanly, and staff trust protector roles with people the family trusts for decades. Scale helps, but continuity helps more.

Third, the migration and the law are now reinforcing each other in a new way. Families once moved to Florida for the tax savings and then discovered the trust law. Now they move for the trust law and stay for the tax savings. The order reversed, and the commitment deepened: a family that moves its situs is harder to lose than a family that moves its residence.

The attorney's client funded the trust. Not because the tax code forced him to, but because the law finally let him build something that would outlast him by centuries, in a place where the professionals, the courts, and the market all exist to support it. That is the new Florida estate plan: less countdown, more horizon.
