The Great Wealth Migration: Why $100 Billion Is Moving to Florida’s Gulf Coast

Florida’s Gulf Coast is absorbing an unprecedented wave of wealth migration. What’s driving it, where the money is landing, and what it means for trust and estate planning.
Prime Domain Capital · October 2026 · 5 min read

In the first quarter of 2024, a partner at a white-shoe New York law firm called a trust company in Naples, Florida, with an unusual request. His client — a tech founder who’d just sold his company for $800 million — wanted to move not just his money but his entire estate plan to Florida. The trusts, the foundations, the family limited partnerships. Everything. “He’s done with New York,” the lawyer said. “Done with the taxes, done with the winters, done with all of it.”

The Naples trust officer had heard this before. She’d heard it a dozen times that month.

Florida’s Gulf Coast — the stretch from Tampa through Sarasota, Fort Myers, and Naples down to Marco Island — is experiencing the most significant wealth migration in American history. The numbers are staggering. IRS migration data consistently shows Florida gaining tens of billions in adjusted gross income annually from high-tax states, with the Gulf Coast capturing a disproportionate share. United Van Lines’ annual migration study has ranked Florida among the top inbound states for a decade. And the wealth isn’t just visiting. It’s putting down roots, hiring trust officers, and restructuring generations of estate plans.

The math that moves millions

The financial case for the migration is brutally simple. Florida has no state income tax, no estate tax, and no inheritance tax. For a high-net-worth individual in California (13.3% top income tax rate) or New York (10.9% top rate, plus New York City’s additional 3.876%), the savings are life-changing. A founder realizing $50 million in capital gains saves roughly $6.6 million in state taxes by being a Florida resident instead of a Californian. That’s not a rounding error. That’s a second home, a foundation endowment, and a trust for the grandkids.

But the tax story, while dominant, isn’t the whole story. Florida’s trust laws are among the most favorable in the country. The state allows directed trusts — where investment, distribution, and administrative functions can be split among different parties. It has a 360-year rule against perpetuities, effectively allowing dynasty trusts that last for generations. Its decanting statute is flexible, allowing trustees to modify irrevocable trusts as circumstances change. For families with complex, multi-generational planning needs, Florida isn’t just a tax haven. It’s a jurisdiction designed for sophisticated wealth management.

Compare this to the states people are leaving. New York’s estate tax cliff — where exceeding the exemption by just 5% subjects the entire estate to tax — has driven estate planners to despair for years. California’s 13.3% income tax applies to trusts administered in the state, creating a powerful incentive to move trust situs. Illinois, New Jersey, Connecticut — the list of high-tax states with aging wealthy populations reads like a client prospect list for every Gulf Coast trust company.

Where the money lands

Not all of Florida is benefiting equally. The migration has a distinct geography, and the Gulf Coast is its epicenter.

Naples, in Collier County, has become the undisputed capital of migrated wealth. The county’s median home price has roughly doubled since 2019, according to local MLS data, driven almost entirely by inbound buyers paying cash. Port Royal, Naples’ most exclusive neighborhood, regularly sees transactions above $20 million. The Naples Winter Wine Festival — the world’s most successful charity wine auction — raised over $30 million in a single weekend in 2024, a figure that would have been unthinkable a decade ago.

Sarasota is the migration’s cultural capital — attracting wealth that wants sophistication without South Beach flash. The city’s investment in the Bay Park, the Sarasota Performing Arts Center, and a thriving arts scene has made it a magnet for Northeasterners who find Naples too quiet and Miami too loud. Manatee County’s population growth has consistently outpaced state averages.

Fort Myers and Lee County represent the migration’s volume play — more affordable than Naples or Sarasota, with explosive growth in master-planned communities like Babcock Ranch, America’s first solar-powered town. Tampa has emerged as the professional’s choice, with a diversifying economy, major corporate relocations, and a downtown renaissance that’s attracted younger wealth alongside the retirees.

What’s striking is the permanence. This isn’t seasonal migration — the “snowbird” pattern of previous decades. IRS data shows the average AGI of inbound Florida migrants has been rising, indicating that it’s not just retirees but working-age high earners making the move. Remote work, accelerated by the pandemic, untethered an entire class of professionals from high-tax jurisdictions. Many aren’t going back.

The trust industry’s Gulf Coast boom

The wealth migration has triggered a corresponding boom in trust and estate services along the Gulf Coast. Independent trust companies — the boutique firms that administer trusts without the conflicts of large banks — have expanded aggressively. Florida Trust Wealth Management, headquartered in the region, has grown to over $5 billion in assets under management. New entrants arrive regularly, drawn by the seemingly inexhaustible supply of clients needing sophisticated fiduciary services.

The competitive dynamics are fascinating. National trust companies (Northern Trust, Bessemer Trust, Chilton Trust) maintain Gulf Coast offices to serve migrated clients. Regional banks (FineMark, Busey) have expanded trust departments. And a new generation of independent firms — often founded by refugees from large institutions — competes on service, flexibility, and local knowledge.

For estate planning attorneys, the migration has been a bonanza. The demand for trust restatements, situs changes, and new Florida-domiciled structures has overwhelmed capacity at many firms. Wait times for initial consultations at top Naples estate planning practices now stretch for months. The bottleneck isn’t demand. It’s qualified professionals.

The counterargument: is it sustainable?

Skeptics raise legitimate questions. Florida’s property insurance crisis — with premiums tripling in some coastal areas and major carriers withdrawing — threatens the cost calculus that drives migration. Hurricane risk is real and growing; Hurricane Ian’s $112 billion in damages in 2022 was a wake-up call. And the very influx that’s driving prosperity is straining infrastructure, from roads to water to healthcare.

There’s also the question of whether the tax advantage is permanent. Federal proposals to limit state tax arbitrage surface periodically. While Florida’s lack of income tax is constitutionally protected at the state level, changes to federal deductibility or new wealth taxes could alter the math.

But the deepest skepticism concerns culture. Can a region absorb this much wealth this quickly without losing the character that attracted people in the first place? Naples residents who remember when Fifth Avenue was sleepy watch luxury towers rise with mixed feelings. The traffic, the construction, the changing demographics — growth has costs, and not everyone is enthusiastic about paying them.

The migrants themselves seem unconcerned. Surveys of recent arrivals consistently show high satisfaction and low intention to return. The combination of tax savings, weather, and lifestyle proves remarkably sticky. People who move for money often stay for the mornings — the ones where it’s 72 degrees in January and the coffee tastes better on a lanai than it ever did in a Manhattan kitchen.

What this means for trust planning

For families considering the move — or trust companies serving those who’ve already made it — three implications stand out.

First, situs matters more than ever. Moving a trust’s administration to Florida isn’t just about the grantor’s residence. It’s about accessing Florida’s favorable trust laws, its experienced fiduciary infrastructure, and its deep bench of estate planning professionals. The migration has created a network effect: the more sophisticated families move, the more sophisticated the local expertise becomes, attracting more families.

Second, timing matters. The professionals are overwhelmed, the real estate is expensive, and the best trust companies have waiting lists. Families who plan methodically — engaging counsel before the move, structuring trusts under Florida law from inception — navigate the transition smoothly. Those who move first and plan later often discover that unwinding a New York or California trust structure is more complex than establishing a Florida one from scratch.

Third, the Gulf Coast’s trust industry is still maturing. The rapid growth has attracted talented professionals but also opportunists. Due diligence on fiduciaries — their experience, their independence, their fee structures — matters enormously. The best trust companies in the region rival anything in New York or Chicago. The worst are selling sunshine and tax savings without the substance to back it up.

The Great Wealth Migration isn’t slowing. If anything, the combination of remote work permanence, state tax differentials, and demographic aging suggests it’s accelerating. Florida’s Gulf Coast spent decades building the infrastructure — legal, financial, cultural — to receive this wealth. Now that it’s arriving, the question isn’t whether the region can handle it. It’s whether the families moving can navigate the transition wisely enough to preserve what they’re bringing.
