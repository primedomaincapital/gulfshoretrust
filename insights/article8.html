How the 2026 Exemption Sunset Changes the Math on Big Gifts

The sunset every estate planner spent five years preparing for did not happen. The $15 million exemption is now permanent, and the families who rushed to give before the deadline need a new plan — and the families who waited need one too.
Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

In the spring of 2025, a couple in Naples sat with their estate planning attorney and signed papers to give $27 million to a dynasty trust for their grandchildren. They had spent two years preparing for it. Their advisers had told them, correctly, that the federal estate tax exemption was scheduled to fall by half on January 1, 2026, from $13.99 million per person to roughly $7 million, and that any exemption not used before the deadline would be lost. They gave early, they gave big, and they gave before the clock ran out. Then, on July 4, 2025, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act was signed into law, and the clock was cancelled. The exemption did not fall. It rose, to $15 million per person, $30 million for a couple, and it became permanent.

The couple’s gift was not a mistake. The trust they funded is a good trust, and the assets inside it are growing outside their taxable estates. But the plan around it was built for a deadline that no longer exists, and the planning that followed, the gifts they did not make, the trusts they did not fund, the structures they chose because they were in a hurry, was built on an assumption the law has now erased. They are not alone. Every family with a taxable estate spent the last five years planning for a sunset. The sunset did not happen, and the math on big gifts has changed in ways that reward the families who understand what actually changed, and punish the ones who keep planning as if the deadline were still coming.

What actually happened

The law is the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025. Section 70106 sets the federal estate and gift tax exemption at $15 million per person, $30 million for a married couple, effective January 1, 2026, indexed for inflation thereafter, and permanent. The top rate stays at 40 percent. The anti-clawback regulation, Treasury Decision 9884, remains in place, which means gifts made under the higher exemption will not be clawed back if the law ever changes and the exemption falls. The sunset that the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act had scheduled for the end of 2025, the fall from $13.99 million to about $7 million, was repealed before it took effect.

The scale of what was averted is worth stating plainly. A couple with a $30 million estate would have faced roughly $9.2 million in federal estate tax under the sunset, at 40 percent on the roughly $23 million above a $7 million combined exemption. Under the new law, the same couple owes nothing at the federal level, because the full $30 million sits inside the exemption. The difference is not a planning nuance. It is the difference between a taxable estate and a non-taxable one, and it applies to every family whose wealth sits between $14 million and $30 million.

The change also ends the urgency that defined estate planning for five years. The use-it-or-lose-it deadline is gone. A family that has not yet used its exemption does not lose it on a date certain, and a family that used it early does not get it back. The exemption is now a resource to be deployed on the family’s schedule, not the tax code’s.

The math on a thirty-million-dollar gift

Consider a couple with $40 million. Under the old sunset plan, they would have rushed to give $27.98 million before January 1, 2026, using both spouses’ $13.99 million exemptions, and paid no gift tax. The remaining $12 million would stay in their estates, and at 40 percent the tax on it would be roughly $4.8 million at the second death, assuming no further growth. Under the new law, the same couple can give $30 million tax-free, leaving $10 million in the estate and a tax of roughly $4 million. The difference is $800,000, and it grows with every year the gifted assets compound inside the trust instead of inside the estate.

The compounding is where the real money is. A $30 million gift placed in a dynasty trust and invested at a 6 percent return doubles in about twelve years, and every dollar of that growth sits outside the taxable estate. A $30 million gift made today is worth roughly $60 million to the grandchildren in twelve years, tax-free. The same $30 million left in the estate, growing at the same rate, is worth the same $60 million, but $30 million of it is above the exemption and taxed at 40 percent, a $12 million bill that the trust never pays. The gift is not just a transfer. It is a decision about where the next twelve years of growth will be taxed.

The generation-skipping transfer tax exemption matches the estate exemption at $15 million per person, so the same $30 million can skip a generation entirely, funding a trust for grandchildren and beyond without a second layer of tax. Florida, where the couple lives, adds no state estate or inheritance tax of its own, which is why the situs decision matters as much as the gift decision. A New York family with the same $40 million faces a state exemption of $7.35 million for 2026 and a state tax on the balance, on top of the federal picture.

What the new law does not fix

The new exemption solves the federal problem for most families. It does not solve the state problem, the basis problem, or the control problem, and a family that mistakes a higher exemption for a finished plan will find those problems waiting.

The state problem is the largest. Twelve states and the District of Columbia still impose their own estate or inheritance taxes, with exemptions far below the federal level. New York’s $7.35 million exemption for 2026 means a $40 million estate owes state tax on roughly $32.65 million, even though the federal bill is zero. Massachusetts, Oregon, and Minnesota have exemptions at or below $2 million. A family that moves to Florida eliminates the state tax entirely, which is one reason the Gulf Coast has absorbed so much migrating wealth, and one reason the situs of the trust matters as much as the situs of the family.

The basis problem is subtler. Assets given during life carry the donor’s cost basis to the recipient, while assets held until death receive a step-up in basis to fair market value. A couple that gives away $30 million of highly appreciated stock saves the estate tax but hands the grandchildren a capital gains bill on decades of appreciation. A couple that holds the same stock until death pays no estate tax under the new exemption and no capital gains tax either, because the basis steps up. The right answer depends on the asset: give away the high-growth, low-basis assets that will appreciate, and hold the low-growth, high-basis assets that will not.

The control problem is the one the article on minority stakes would recognize. A gift is irrevocable. A couple that gives $30 million to a dynasty trust cannot take it back if the business needs capital, if a child divorces, or if the market falls. The trust can be drafted with flexibility, a trust protector, decanting provisions, a power to substitute assets, but the flexibility is in the drafting, not in the gift. The families who give the most need the most careful drafting, and the drafting should happen before the gift, not after.

The case for waiting — and why it fails

The counterargument deserves a hearing, because the new law seems to remove the reason to hurry. The exemption is permanent. There is no deadline. A family can wait, watch the law, and give when it is convenient. The urgency was artificial, the argument goes, and now that it is gone, the rational move is to hold the assets, keep the control, and let the step-up in basis do its work at death.

The counterargument fails on the compounding, not the deadline. Every year a family waits to give is a year of growth that stays inside the taxable estate instead of moving outside it. A $30 million gift made today and growing at 6 percent is worth $60 million in twelve years, all of it outside the estate. A $30 million gift made in twelve years is worth $30 million, and the $30 million of growth that happened in the meantime is inside the estate, taxed at 40 percent. The deadline is gone. The cost of waiting is not.

There is also a political risk the counterargument ignores. The exemption is permanent in the sense that no sunset is scheduled. It is not permanent in the sense that Congress cannot change it. A future Congress could lower it, and the anti-clawback regulation means gifts already made are protected, while gifts not yet made are not. The families who gave before the deadline that never came are protected by the regulation they did not need. The families who wait are betting that the law will never change, which is a bet the last decade of tax legislation does not support.

What to do before year-end

The practical work is straightforward, and it starts with a number. Every family with more than $15 million per person should know exactly how much exemption it has used, how much remains, and what the estate tax would be at today’s values and at the values the assets will reach in ten years. The second step is to decide what to give: the high-growth assets first, the low-basis assets last, and the assets the family may need back never. The third step is to fund the dynasty trust, in Florida, where the situs is favorable, the duration runs a thousand years, and the trustee is down the street.

The families who rushed to give before the sunset should review what they did, not regret it. The trusts they funded are good trusts, and the exemption they used is protected. What they should do now is finish the plan: use the remaining exemption, review the drafting for the flexibility they skipped in the rush, and make sure the trust they funded in haste is the trust they would fund today. The sunset did not happen. The planning it forced was not wasted. It was early, and early, in estate planning, is usually right.
