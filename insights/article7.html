Florida vs. Delaware vs. South Dakota: Where Should a Dynasty Trust Live?

One state offers forever. One offers the deepest bench of trust companies on earth. One offers a thousand years and a trustee down the street. The right answer depends on what the family is actually buying.
Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

In 2021, a series of leaked files known as the Pandora Papers showed the world something the trust industry had known for forty years: South Dakota, a state of fewer than a million people, had become one of the great vaults of global wealth. Of the 206 U.S.-based trusts identified in the Pandora Papers — holding more than $1 billion combined — 81 were based in South Dakota, the most of any state, according to the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists. The reporting also tied some South Dakota trusts to people and companies accused of human rights abuses and other wrongdoing. The attention was unwelcome. The underlying fact was not in dispute. South Dakota’s trust industry had done something extraordinary, and every family with a dynasty trust to place has had to answer the same question since: why South Dakota, and not Florida, and not Delaware?

The numbers explain the attention. Assets in South Dakota trusts reached about $815 billion at the end of 2024, according to the state Division of Banking, and roughly $906 billion at the end of 2025 — a $91 billion increase in a single year, the highest dollar amount in state history, reported by South Dakota Public Broadcasting in May 2026. The state had 114 trust companies at the end of 2025, and the industry has grown by roughly $100 billion a year since 2018.

But scale is not a recommendation. The situs decision — the choice of which state’s law governs the trust — turns on four questions: how long the trust can last, what tax it pays, how well it protects assets, and who administers it. The three leading states answer those questions differently, and the differences matter more than the rankings suggest.

Duration: forever, a thousand years, or 110

South Dakota’s advantage starts with a single sentence. In 1983, it became the first state to abolish the Rule Against Perpetuities — the centuries-old rule that forced trusts to terminate after a certain period. Its statute, SDCL 43-5-8, states plainly that the common-law rule against perpetuities is not in force in the state. A trust created in South Dakota can last forever. There is no cap to plan around, no savings clause to draft, no date on which the structure must unwind.

Delaware is perpetual for personal property — securities, cash, business interests held through entities — and capped at 110 years for real property held directly in trust, under Del. Code Tit. 25, section 503. Holding real estate through an LLC inside the trust effectively sidesteps the limit, which is why the 110-year cap rarely binds a well-advised family. For a trust funded with a portfolio, Delaware is, for practical purposes, perpetual too.

Florida is the newcomer to long duration, and its rule has a date attached that families ignore at their peril. For trusts created on or after July 1, 2022, Florida law allows a trust to last 1,000 years under Fla. Stat. section 689.225(2)(g). Trusts created between January 1, 2001 and June 30, 2022 are capped at 360 years. Trusts created before 2001 run 90 years, or 21 years after a life in being. Florida has not repealed the rule against perpetuities. It has extended it, twice, and which period applies depends entirely on when the trust was created. A 1,000-year cap is very long, but it is a cap — and a trust whose drafter included a conventional perpetuities savings clause ends when that clause says it ends, not in a thousand years. For a family that will rewrite the trust through decanting every generation or two — and every long trust is rewritten, because no drafter can foresee the needs of descendants ten generations out — the difference between perpetual and 1,000 years is theoretical.

Tax: three states, one answer, different fine print

On state income tax, the three states converge. South Dakota imposes no state income tax on trust income. Florida charges no state income tax on private trusts — the operative authority is statutory, Fla. Stat. section 220.03(1)(e), which excludes private trusts from the definition of “corporation” taxed under Chapter 220, not the state constitution, which bars a tax only on the income of natural persons. Delaware imposes a state income tax but exempts trust income where the beneficiaries are not Delaware residents, which covers the ordinary dynasty trust created by an out-of-state family.

That convergence hides a trap. The Supreme Court’s unanimous 2019 decision in North Carolina Department of Revenue v. Kimberley Rice Kaestner 1992 Family Trust, 588 U.S. 262, held that the presence of in-state beneficiaries alone does not empower a state to tax trust income that has not been distributed to beneficiaries who have no right to demand it and are uncertain ever to receive it. The Court expressly declined to decide taxes premised on the settlor’s residence, taxes using beneficiary residence as one of several combined factors, and taxes reaching noncontingent beneficiaries. California, which taxes the entire income of a trust whenever a noncontingent California-resident beneficiary exists, is the live example of what the Court left open.

The planning conclusion is not “move to South Dakota.” It is draft the beneficiaries’ interests as fully discretionary. Discretion, not geography, is the lever that keeps an out-of-state beneficiary’s residence from creating tax nexus — a point Jose M. Lorenzo Jr. makes at length in his 2026 analysis of Florida dynasty trusts.

Protection: where the states actually diverge

Asset protection is where the ranking separates. Steve Oshins, of the Las Vegas firm Oshins & Associates, compared state laws for Financial Advisor magazine and ranked the ten states that provide the most protection for dynasty trusts. South Dakota ranked first, with full protection. Nevada ranked second. Delaware tied for seventh. Florida ranked tenth — and the reason matters: Florida’s trusts are not protected against divorcing spouse claims or for child support claims, and neither are Delaware’s.

The mechanics behind the ranking are statutory. South Dakota allows self-settled domestic asset protection trusts: a person can create a trust for his own benefit and shield it from his own creditors, with a two-year lookback for existing and future creditors. Delaware’s Qualified Dispositions in Trust Act allows the same structure but with a four-year lookback for creditors whose claims arose after the transfer — one of the longer waiting periods among the top jurisdictions. Florida does not recognize self-settled asset protection trusts at all. A Floridian cannot create a trust for his own benefit and shield it from his own creditors the way a South Dakotan or a Nevadan can.

That gap is real, and it is narrower than the rankings suggest. The protection that matters most to a dynasty trust is protection for beneficiaries, not for the settlor — and Florida’s discretionary trust protections for beneficiaries are strong. A properly drafted discretionary trust keeps assets out of a beneficiary’s marital property in divorce, and Florida has not enacted any statute voiding irrevocable trust provisions on dissolution. The family that needs self-settled protection for the founder himself has a reason to leave Florida. The family that needs multi-generational protection for descendants mostly does not.

The trustee market: depth against proximity

Delaware’s advantage is not a statute. It is an industry. Delaware’s trust business is the oldest and deepest of the three, built on a Court of Chancery that has decided trust disputes for more than two centuries and a bar that writes the law other states copy. The scale shows up in the bank data: Delaware banks managed $97.3 billion in personal trust and agency assets across 28,981 accounts at December 31, 2025, according to Federal Financial Institutions Examination Council Call Report data, with an average managed account of $3,359,004 — the largest average account size in the country. South Dakota banks managed $57.0 billion across 21,690 accounts, an average of $2,629,890. Those figures cover bank trust departments only, but they measure something real.

South Dakota’s market is younger and more specialized: 114 trust companies at the end of 2025, many of them boutiques built specifically for directed trusts and dynasty structures, administering $906 billion between them. Trusts & Estates magazine has ranked South Dakota the number one trust jurisdiction in the nation, and the ranking reflects a deliberate strategy: the state has spent four decades writing statutes that attract trust business, from the 1983 perpetuities repeal to its directed-trust and privacy provisions. South Dakota trust records are generally private, and some trust court records can be sealed — a privacy framework no other leading state matches.

Florida’s trustee market is different in kind. It is a market of proximity: the trust companies and bank trust departments of Naples, Sarasota, and Miami serve families who live there, hold property there, and want a trustee they can meet. Florida’s directed trust act, Fla. Stat. sections 736.1401 through 736.1416, lets a family split investment, distribution, and administrative functions among different parties, so a Gulf Coast family can keep investment authority with its own adviser while a Florida corporate trustee handles administration. For a family whose wealth, property, and beneficiaries are in Florida, that combination — local trustee, directed structure, no state income tax, a thousand-year term — is difficult to beat on convenience, and convenience compounds over decades of administration.

The case for staying home

Here is the counterargument, and it comes from Florida’s own practitioners. For most Florida families, leaving the state buys very little and costs something real. Florida already offers a 1,000-year duration, no state income tax on private trusts, a modern directed trust act, and one of the stronger decanting statutes in the country. The remaining gap between Florida and South Dakota is perpetual duration and self-settled asset protection — and only the second of those is likely to matter to a given family.

There is also a cost to distance that never appears in a ranking. A South Dakota situs means a South Dakota trustee, South Dakota administration, and a family that must manage a relationship with a fiduciary a thousand miles away, in a state where it holds no property and knows no one. The families that choose an out-of-state situs and then neglect the relationship — the trustee who never meets the beneficiaries, the trust protector who is never appointed, the decanting that never happens — have bought the best statute and the worst administration.

Where each state wins

The framework, then, is straightforward. South Dakota wins when the family needs self-settled asset protection, truly perpetual duration, and maximum privacy — and when the assets are large enough, often cited as $50 million and up, to justify a specialist trustee at a distance. Delaware wins when the family wants the deepest institutional bench, the most sophisticated directed-trust practice, and a court system that has answered every trust question at least once. Florida wins when the family, the property, and the beneficiaries are already in Florida — when the trustee can sit across the table, the directed structure keeps investment decisions with the family’s adviser, and a thousand years is long enough for any plan that will be rewritten a dozen times before it gets there.

For the Gulf Coast family that is the subject of this site, the answer is usually Florida, and the reason is not patriotism. It is that the marginal benefit of forever over a thousand years is zero for a trust that will be decanted, the marginal benefit of Delaware’s bench is small for a trust administered by a competent Florida trustee, and the benefit of a trustee down the street — who knows the family, the property, and the local courts — is large and grows with every year the trust runs. The best situs is the one the family will actually tend. A dynasty trust is a thousand-year promise. It should live where someone is watching it.
