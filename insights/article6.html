What Does a Florida Dynasty Trust Cost to Set Up and Run?

The drafting fee is the smallest number in the engagement. The recurring bill — trustee, tax return, administration — runs every year, for centuries.
Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The invoice that surprised the family was not the first one. It was the fourth. A Naples couple in their late fifties had funded a $6 million dynasty trust in 2023, paid their attorney’s fee without blinking, and filed the gift tax return on time. Then the annual statements began: a trustee fee, a fiduciary tax return, a charge for the rental property the trust held, a setup fee they had forgotten was coming. None of it was large. All of it was permanent. The trust, their attorney had told them, could run for a thousand years. So, apparently, could the billing.

That is the honest shape of dynasty trust cost in Florida. The drafting fee is a one-time number, large enough to notice and small enough to forget. The recurring cost is the real price, and it is the number most families never see itemized before they sign. A dynasty trust is not a document. It is a small institution, and institutions have payrolls.

The setup bill: $8,000 to $25,000, and what moves it

Drafting a Florida dynasty trust with proper generation-skipping transfer tax planning typically costs between $8,000 and $25,000 in attorney fees, according to published practitioner ranges, depending on complexity, the asset types being transferred, and whether the attorney also handles the gift tax return. Simpler dynasty structures start near $10,000; highly customized ones — business interests, multiple properties, special provisions — run past $25,000. The American College of Trust and Estate Counsel’s guidelines put attorney fees for a comprehensive trust for a high-net-worth individual with complex assets, including business interests and multiple real estate holdings, routinely at $5,000 to $20,000 or more, with the most complex plans reaching $20,000 to $40,000 before a single asset is transferred.

What moves the number is design, not paperwork. A cash-and-securities gift is straightforward. A sale to an intentionally defective grantor trust for a promissory note, with a valuation of discounted LLC interests, is a different engagement entirely — and involves a qualified appraiser as a separate cost. That is the framing Jose M. Lorenzo Jr., a Florida estate planning attorney whose firm published a full cost breakdown reviewed in September 2026, gives his clients: the fee turns on how the trust is funded, what is going in, how much flexibility is built in, and whether a gift tax return has to be prepared with the generation-skipping exemption allocated. A misallocation of that exemption, he notes, is one of the few errors in this area that is effectively permanent. Multi-state property adds further cost: each state where the family holds real property requires separate legal review and retitling work, and total setup costs for a comprehensive multi-state plan can reach $25,000 to $40,000.

The annual bill: the trustee takes the largest share

Recurring trustee and tax-preparation costs run for as long as the trust does, and they are the costs that determine whether a dynasty trust makes sense at a given funding level. Lorenzo Law’s published ranges, drawn from publicly available trustee fee schedules, give the clearest itemization available for Florida:

A corporate trustee at a regional institution charges around 1.25% annually on the first $1 million, stepping down toward 0.75% above $3 million, with fees negotiated above roughly $6 million. An independent or boutique trust company runs roughly 0.60% on the first $2 million, declining to 0.30% above $10 million — paired with a much higher minimum. The minimum annual fee is around $3,000 for an irrevocable trust at a regional institution, and substantially higher at independents. A directed trust, in which the family keeps investment authority with a trust director and the corporate trustee is directed rather than discretionary, is priced lower than a fully discretionary one: one published schedule runs 0.50% against 0.60% at the first tier.

Other published ranges agree on the shape. Corporate trustee fees generally run 0.5% to 1% of trust assets per year, with minimums often around $3,000 to $5,000 annually; a trust holding $5 million in assets could therefore carry $25,000 to $50,000 in annual administration costs. On a $10 million trust, the range widens to $50,000 to $150,000 a year at 0.5% to 1.5%. At 1% annually over thirty years, a $10 million trust pays $3 million or more in trustee fees before accounting for any investment drag on the assets used to pay those fees.

The minimum fee is the number that matters at smaller funding levels. On a trust funded with $400,000, a $3,000 minimum is 0.75% a year, every year, regardless of what the percentage schedule says — plus a Form 1041 each year. If the federal tax benefit at that funding level is zero, the family is paying a permanent drag for asset protection and control alone. That is a defensible reason to do it. It is not a tax reason.

The smaller lines that add up

The fiduciary income tax return, Form 1041, runs around $1,000, and more with closely held K-1s, multiple states, or an ESBT election. Real property held in trust costs around $1,000 per year per property, plus about $1,000 per purchase or sale. Account setup runs around $1,500 one time.

Then there is the tax the trust itself pays, which is not a fee but behaves like one. Trust income is taxed at a combined top marginal rate of 40.8% above just $16,000 in 2026 — the 37% top bracket plus the 3.8% net investment income tax, both hitting at $16,000 of retained income. A single individual does not reach the 37% bracket until $640,600. That compressed schedule is why most dynasty trusts are drafted as grantor trusts, with the grantor paying the income tax personally — which is itself a cost, just one that shows up on the grantor’s return rather than the trust’s.

There are also the costs that arrive disguised as something else. Moving a Florida homestead into an irrevocable trust for descendants resets the Save Our Homes assessment cap — a real, immediate cost for a speculative future benefit. Funding the trust with illiquid assets means valuation fees at funding and again at every distribution. An individual trustee is cheaper — a family member charges nothing or reasonable compensation — but asks one person to administer, file, and account for decades, then to have a successor ready. Many families use both: a corporate trustee for administration and a family trust director for investments.

A worked example: the $5 million trust, year one and year ten

Take a $5 million trust, funded with marketable securities, administered by a regional corporate trustee at 1% with a $3,000 minimum, with a straightforward Form 1041. Year one: drafting at the midpoint of the published range, say $15,000; account setup $1,500; trustee fee $50,000; tax return $1,000. All-in first year: roughly $67,500, of which $52,500 recurs.

Year ten, assuming the assets have grown at 5% net to about $7.7 million and the trustee fee has stepped down toward 0.75%: the annual bill is roughly $58,000 in trustee fees plus $1,000 for the return — about $59,000 a year, every year, for a structure whose tax benefit depends entirely on the family’s estate exceeding the exemption. The federal estate, gift, and generation-skipping exemption is $15 million per person in 2026 — $30 million per married couple — made permanent by P.L. 119-21. A family worth $5 million has no federal estate tax problem at all. The trust is buying governance and protection, not tax savings, and the family should know which one it is buying before it signs.

At $3 million to $5 million in transferred assets, most practitioners consider the structure genuinely cost-efficient — the point at which the administration fees are small relative to the tax savings. Below $1 million, the minimum fees and the fixed return cost consume the benefit. Between those poles sits the judgment call — creditor exposure, a blended family, a beneficiary with a spending problem — where protection, not tax, justifies the price.

The comparison that makes the cost look small

In Florida, probate costs are set by a statutory fee schedule: attorney compensation in formal administration is presumed reasonable at 3% on the value between $100,000 and $1 million, stepping down through 2.5% and 2% bands to 1.5% above $5 million up to $10 million and 1% above that, under Fla. Stat. section 733.6171. A $10 million estate probated in Florida generates nearly $195,000 in attorney fees alone on that schedule, before personal representative compensation and costs — and probate repeats at every generation. A dynasty trust, properly funded, pays its drafting fee once and never probates the assets again. Against a single probate, the trust looks expensive. Against three probates across three generations, it looks cheap.

The stronger counterargument is the tax arithmetic. Lorenzo Law’s own illustration: a married couple transfers $15 million to a dynasty trust in 2026, using half their combined $30 million exemption, with assets netting 4% a year after costs and distributions. Held outright and taxed at 40% at each generation, the family keeps $102.4 million by the third generation and pays $41.6 million in transfer tax. Held in the trust, it keeps $284.2 million and pays nothing. The difference is $181.8 million on a $15 million transfer — larger than the tax actually paid, because every dollar paid in tax also stops compounding for the remaining decades.

Both counterarguments are real, and both have the same limit: they describe families with taxable estates. For the family below the exemption, the probate comparison still holds — a trust avoids probate — but the $181.8 million illustration does not apply, because there was no tax to avoid. The cost question is therefore not “is a dynasty trust expensive?” It is “expensive relative to what, for this family, at this funding level?”

What to budget before you sign

Three rules follow. First, budget the recurring number, not the drafting fee: take the trustee’s published schedule, apply it to the funding amount, add the Form 1041, add $1,000 per property held, and multiply by ten. If that ten-year number surprises you, the trust is too small for the structure or the structure is too grand for the trust. Second, price the directed option: keeping investment authority with a family trust director under Florida’s Uniform Directed Trust Act cuts the trustee’s fee at the first tier and keeps the family’s investment judgment in the family’s hands. Third, be honest about which benefit you are buying. If the estate will not approach $15 million per person, say so, and decide whether asset protection and governance are worth roughly 1% a year, every year, for as long as the trust runs.

The Naples couple from the opening now budgets $60,000 a year for their trust and considers it money well spent — their estate is large enough that the tax arithmetic works, and the trust holds a business interest that needs a governance structure anyway. They do not regret the trust. They regret that nobody showed them the fourth invoice before the first one. The drafting fee is the price of the document. The annual bill is the price of the institution. Know both before you sign.
